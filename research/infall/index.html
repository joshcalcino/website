How infalling gas reshapes planet-forming discs

From Anatomy of a Fall to AB Aurigae

Protoplanetary discs are discs of gas and dust that surround young stars, and are the birthplaces of planets. In the classic picture of star and planet formation, protoplanetary discs form as an outcome of the conservation of angular momentum from an infalling envelope. It was thought that once this envelope completed infalling (within ~500kyr), the connection between the protostar and protoplanetary disc with the surrounding environment was severed. However, recent observations reveal that even relatively old systems (ages > 1Myr) are still actively accreting from the environment. How this infalling material impacts the structures and properties of protoplanetary discs is crucial for understanding the resulting impacts on the planet formation process, and whether infalling gas can also produce similar substructures as planets do.

What happens when infalling gas impacts the disc?

In Anatomy of a Fall (named after a French movie with the same name) my collaborators and I used three-dimensional smooth particle hydrodynamic (SPH) simulations to follow a narrow stream of gas, known as a streamer, as it collides with the disc. We found that the infalling streamer can indeed lead to some interesting phenomena.

We found that spiral arms launched by infall can look deceptively similar to those generated by multi-Jupiter mass planets.

The encounter launched spiral waves and pushed some gas onto stretched-out orbits.

The spiral patterns behaved in surprising ways. Some barely moved, even while gas flowed through them; others travelled faster than gas on a circular orbit at the same distance from the star. Tracking how spirals move could therefore help identify infall. The effects also outlasted the initial encounter: in our simulation, the disc remained slightly elongated for around 100,000 years.

Following the gas around AB Aurigae

AB Aurigae is a young star surrounded by a disc with striking spiral arms. It is also receiving fresh gas. In an observational study led by Jessica Speedie, which I coauthored, we traced three streams towards the disc and identified a region where two of them appear to merge with it.

Telescopes can measure gas motion as well as its location. Small shifts in the wavelength of light reveal whether gas is moving towards or away from us. Maps of that motion around AB Aur show bends and wiggles, rather than the smooth pattern expected from gas travelling in circular orbits.

These disturbances had been interpreted as evidence for gravitational instability: a disc so massive that its own gravity helps gather gas into spiral arms. In our follow-up paper on AB Aur, we asked whether infall could produce similar signals. Our simulations recreated key features of the disturbed gas motion without including the gravity of the gas itself.

This means that those signals alone cannot establish whether a disc is gravitationally unstable. Infall offers another explanation. The two processes could also happen together, so our result does not rule out gravitational instability.

Connecting the spirals and the dust ring

We also explored a model combining infall with a second star orbiting inside the disc's central gap. Together, they reproduced many features of AB Aur's spiral pattern and dust ring. Understanding how discs respond to their surroundings helps us interpret the structures we see in them. Before attributing a spiral to an unseen planet or to the disc's own gravity, we need to consider the gas arriving from outside.
