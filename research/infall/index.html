From theory to application: Modeling infall around AB Aurigae

Protoplanetary discs are discs of gas and dust that surround young stars, and are the birthplaces of planets. In the classic picture of star and planet formation, protoplanetary discs form as an outcome of the conservation of angular momentum from an infalling envelope. It was thought that once this envelope completed infalling (within ~500kyr), the connection between the protostar and protoplanetary disc with the surrounding environment was severed. However, recent observations reveal that even relatively old systems (ages > 1Myr) are still actively accreting from the environment. How this infalling material impacts the structures and properties of protoplanetary discs is crucial for understanding the resulting impacts on the planet formation process, and whether infalling gas can also produce similar substructures as planets do.

What happens when infalling gas impacts the disc?

In Anatomy of a Fall (named after a French movie with the same name) my collaborators and I used three-dimensional smooth particle hydrodynamic (SPH) simulations to follow a narrow stream of gas, known as a streamer, as it collides with the disc. We found that the infalling streamer can indeed lead to some interesting phenomena. Below is a movie which shows an example of the simulations. You can see that when the streamer hits the disc, a lot of spiral arms are generated, and they rotate around the disc with different pattern speeds.

We found that spiral arms launched by infall can look deceptively similar to those generated by multi-Jupiter mass planets. The figure below shows a comparison between the spiral arms seen in another one of my simulations compared with spiral arms launched by a multi-Jupiter mass planet from Dong et al. 2015. Although these spiral arms look very similar, there is a way we can tell them apart. Spiral arms launched by a planet should move with the same pattern speed as the planet itself. So if the planet takes 100 years to make a single orbit, so do the spiral arms. But we found that infall driven spirals do not have such simple pattern speeds: some spirals can be stationary on ~1000 year timescales, while other spiral arms move on much shorter timescales.

By utilizing multi-epoch scattered light observations spanning multi-year timescales, it is actually possible to measure how quickly spiral arms move. This has actually been done in observations of a disc around a young star known as MWC 758. The observations suggest the spiral arms in this system are moving much slower than we would expect for the previously proposed planet candidates. A more distant planet is still possible. Perhaps this is suggesting that infall is at play in this system!

Modeling infall around AB Aurigae

AB Aurigae is a young star surrounded by a disc with spectacular spiral arms, located about 450 light years from Earth. It never ceases to amaze me that we are able to image these distant systems with such exquisite detail! This disc is gigantic. The bright but diffuse looking ring you see in the image below is located about 2 times the distance from the Sun to Neptune in our own Solar System. The spiral structures you see outside this ring are even more distant, hundreds of times the distance between the Earth and the Sun!

Recent observations have shown that the disc around AB Aurigae is actively accreting material from its surrounding environment through infalling streamers. The observations, which used the Atamaca Large Millimeter Array (ALMA), targeted CO line emission. Line emission is particularly useful when imaging protoplanetary discs since they can be used to measure Doppler shifts, meaning we can reconstruct the velocity field of the infalling material and of the disc. The observations confirm the infalling nature of this material, while observations within the disc itself show peculiar gas flows associated with spiral arms (see below). In particular, a converging velocity flow is seen around one of the spiral arms. These perturbations had been interpreted as evidence for gravitational instability: a disc so massive that its own gravity helps gather gas into spiral arms.

In our follow-up paper on AB Aur, we explored what signatures infall would produce in the gas velocity field. Our simulations recreated key features of the perturbed gas velocity field without including the gravity of the gas itself. This means that the signals in the observations cannot directly establish that this disc is gravitationally unstable, since the observed infalling material can plausibly recreate similar signals. The two processes could also happen together, so our result does not rule out gravitational instability.

Connecting infall with potential companions

In Poblete et al. 2020, we showed that a binary companion inside the cavity of AB Aurigae could explain a lot of the observations of perturbed gas kinematics and spiral arms in the cavity. Since then, our understanding of AB Aurigae has evolved and we now know of the ongoing infall. Therefore, I revisited our old model of AB Aurigae and added some infall. The result below is quite compelling: The match with the observations somewhat speaks for itself! Our hypothesized companion has not been detected yet, and there is a bunch of debate in the literature about whether there is a planet in this system (known as AB Aur b). But no companion is really truly confirmed yet in my humble opinion. Hopefully in the not too distant future we will have a clearer picture!
